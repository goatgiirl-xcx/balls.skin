1. Phonology

Baphian phonology is very simple and straight forward. It has a CVN syllable structure, C being any consonant except for the dorsal nasal, optionally geminated or followed by /w/ or /j/. V is any vowel, short or long, high or low tone. N is any nasal.

[ ] denotes the romanization, otherwise the IPA is the romanization. () denotes what letter of the Noellastani alphabet is used. I'll use () later for any Baphian writing I need to show.

Baphian has phonemic consonant and vowel length, this is shown in both the romanization and in Baphian writing by doubling the letter that is being lengthened. In the romanization, geminated sh (Ś) and zh (Ž) are written as ssh and zzh instead.

Baphian has 2 tones, high and low tone, this is also known as pitch accent. Baphian pitch accent is allophonic with stress as well, with stress being more specifically allophonic with the high pitch. In the IPA, the high pitch ([´]) follows the vowel, and with the romanization having several different ways to show pitch. The most common way is overlining the syllable that is pitched up, and that's what I use the vast majority of the time and will use throughout this document, but underlining, and bolding also work where overlining isn't an option, with low pitch being left alone.

2. Grammar

Baphian is an agglutanative language and uses suffixes to decline words. Meaning that pieces of grammar get attached (or glued) to the end of a word, without much variation. What variation there is still holds information, specifically animacy, which I'll talk about later. Baphian grammar all things considered is very regular. Much of what this means is that the original word doesn't change at all despite new meaning being attached by the bits of grammar being attached to it. This means that you won't have to remember a lot of different forms of words, just the bit of grammar and where it goes on to the original word, most of the time just being the end of the word.

2.1 Plurality

Baphian has a very simple plurality system, and it inflects for 3 numbers; singular, dual, and plural. As with a lot of things in Baphian, you just add a suffix to the end of the world to make it dual or plural ("" yo for the dual and "" we for the plural respectively).

2.2 Cases

Baphian has 7 cases, nominative, accusative, dative, genetive, locative, ablative, and the vocative. All of these opporate how you'd expect them to if you know how they work for the most part, except for the vocative, which I'll explain later. I'll explain what these cases mean for those who don't know, and to be more explicit for what it means for Baphian for those who do. These can go on nouns and adjectives.

Q: Why can adjectives take cases? A: In Baphian, nouns and adjectives are essentially the same type of word, with Baphian really only having a difference between nouns with adjectives and verbs. I will explain in better detail later on, put a pin in it...

The nominative is the subject of the sentence. The subject is the word doing the verb of the sentence generally. I'm using that language very delibratly, because in Baphian, there is technically more than one kind of sentence, and the nominative isn't always used in this way, same with the other cases. These odd use cases will come up and be explained as needed. All postpositions are also automatically in the nominative, never becoming another case.

The accusative is the direct object of a sentence. The direct object is the word that the verb is being directly done to in the sentence generally. It is written as "", ge in the animate and ze in the inanimate.

You may see the accusative twice on some words, this will be explained later.

The dative is the indirect object of the sentence. The indirect object is the word that can change what exactly it does depending on the sentence, but for the most part denotes recipients, or audiences, of the direct object. In Baphian the dative can be used for that as well as marking agents in the passive voice. It is written as "", we in the animate and she in the inanimate.

The genetive is used for words that possess something. It can not be used for an attributive, the vocative takes that use, but I'll explain that later. What this means is that the genetive only marks possession and nothing else. It is written as "", ke in the animate and inanimate, the only case not to change depending on animacy.

The locative is used to indicate location at a place or movement towards a place. It can be translated into English as "in", "at", "on", "towards", or "into" depending on what fits better. It is written as "", jan in the animate and an in the inanimate. The locative can be used in more interesting constructions, namely topic marking, as I'll talk about when I talk about that.

The ablative is used to mark movements away from something, it can be translated into English as "away from", "out of", or "from" depending on what fits better. It is written as "", je in the animate and ce in the inanimate.

2.2.1 Vocative

The vocative in Baphian can seem complicated and daunting, but it honestly is pretty simple once you know how it works. The vocative has 3 use cases; as an ordinary vocative, a way to address someone or get their attention; as an attributive marker and in derivations; as a deictic marker corresponding to English "this" or "that". When used for deixis or as an attributive, the vocative can stack cases before it, following all other cases before it. By default adjectives are in the vocative in the attributive use case before the noun the modify. It is written as "", mu in the animate and nu in the inanimate.

2.3 Topic marking

Baphian has two ways to make a sentence; topic marking, and subject marking. Subject marking is more or less what you'd expect, you mark the subject by doing basically nothing, and make the sentence as you normally would with the nominative accusative etc.. But with topic marking, you mark the topic. What's the difference? It's mostly semantic, but in Baphian, it does make a significant difference to the voice of the sentence, which is a major thing for me personally.

Topic marking is done by putting the topic at the start of the sentence, followed by the appropriate counter word, which i will talk more about later, and you put the counter into the locative case. This more or less translates to "as for topic...".

Example:
"    " /jä´.kän.nu so̞n.än ʑo̞.jo̞´ zɛ̝.pu´.gɛ̝ tɛ̝.dɛ̝´/ (shoe-VOC CL[clothes]-LOC it-DU pretty-ACC be-GNO) meaning "speaking of those shoes, they are pretty".

2.4 Nouns and adjectives

You remember that pin from later? About how nouns and adjectives can take case? I'll explain that now. In Baphian, nouns and adjectives are treated one in the same. How do we differantiate the two? Well, usually by context and the modifying usage of the vocative that I explained earlier. To put it in another way, in Baphian, red isn't necessarily just the adjective describing something as the colour red, it's also the noun 'red one/thing'. And this brings me to a very interesting quirk of Baphian that I personally love a whole lot.

In Baphian, you can turn *any* noun into an adjective, simply by slapping the vocative to the end and putting it in front of whatever you're trying to get it to modify. For example, in Baphian there isn't a word for hairy necessarily, you can make one just by adding the vocative to the word for hair. And for adjectives, they are functionally identical as animate nouns, including declining as such, and due to that, adjectives in attributive use case need the vocative ending. Adjectives without the vocative are usually interpreted as “the ___ one”, or “an object that is ___”. I hope you get what I'm trying to say with this. Basically, anything can be used to describe anything. And I truly do mean that, including verbs. While verbs can't just be turned into adjectives without being turned into nouns first, which I'll explain how to later, you can use any word to describe any other word.

Example:
In Baphian, the 'hairy red (one, thing)' ( ) and the 'red hair' ( ) are made simply by switching the head noun around.

2.4.1 Animacy

Animacy in baphian is very important. It is the only thing that change what case endings you use for a word, and the intricacies of what is considered animate and inanimate will be explained here, as well as what endings each use. Any good dictionary for the baphian language would distinguish the two without hesitation.

What is considered animate

The obvious things that are considered animate are living things, people, animals, plants, insects, bacteria, the works. But nature itself is considered animate as well, as it's constantly changing and evolving and very much alive. Think the tide, it's constantly moving from high to low. The things that make living things work, think cells or organs constantly working to keep you alive. This is largely what seperates animate from inanimate in Baphian, if something is alive or moves then it's animate, well, for the most part. One quirks of this system is that anything being controlled by magic or is undead, is automatically turned inanimate in Baphian. I'll explain why in the next section. Another being every single adjective is treated as animate due to all adjectives being largely treated like weird nouns, whether the object it's supposed to be is actually animate or not.

What is considered inanimate

Obviously furniture and buildings, toys and food, bits and bobs, are all inanimate. Largely what you would expect to be inanimate is. Dead things, including undead things, are considered inanimate. This is due to a deeply rooted belief in Baphian culture that the dead need to be treated with respect largely as much as they would expect people to treat their things, that being incredibly well. Many esoteric concepts and things are considered inanimate as well for similar reasons, but this isn't strict.

2.4.2 Pronouns

Pronouns in Baphian are for the most part optional, Baphian speakers don't need use personal pronouns. They decline irregularly and take both number and case. In Baphian, there are 4 grammatical persons that pronouns use; 1st person, 2nd perosn, 3rd, and 4th person. While 1st through 3rd person do not need to be explained, as they are indifferent from normal, 4th person will need to be explained.

The 4th person is more or less an impersonal pronoun, and in Baphian is commonly used as a general "you". While more common, another way is as a pronoun for the reader of a text, in a way, a 4th wall-breaking pronoun. In some registers, primarily the religious register, and dialects, they become more of a subtle, less important to the conversation, obviate 3rd person pronoun.

2.5 Verbs

In Baphian, verbs are likely one of the most complicated and in depth aspects of the whole language, holding a large amount of information that a sentence in Baphian would hold, such as when the sentence takes place, the tense, where the sentence takes place in time, the aspect, whether the verb is actually even happening or not, the positive and negative, and the relationship between the subject/topic and the verb, the voice.

2.5.1 Positive and negative

The positive and the negative are very self explanitory. In Baphian, if a verb isn't happening, you simply add  "le" to the end.

 "le" isn't normally said as "le", this is a quirk of how Baphian verbs evolved.

2.5.2 Tense

Baphian has 4 tenses, the gnomic, the past, the present, and the future. These all seem incredibly self explainitory, and they absoloutely are what you'd expect them to mean, except for maybe the present and gnomic, which I will explain when they come up.

The past is written as  "de".

The present tense isn't marked, meaning that by default verbs are in the present tense. The present in Baphian isn't just the present moment, but a few moments into the past as well. The exact amount of time shouldn't matter, but it shouldn't be any more than 15 minutes into the past. This is more of a personal and stylistic choice, and usually it will be what you expect.

The future is written as  "di".

2.5.2.1 Gnomic

The gnomic is written as  "de". In Baphian, the gnomic tense is used for things that aren’t set in a specific time, such as general truths or (generally) facts, such as talking about history or science.

2.5.3 Aspects

Aspects affect the verb no matter the tense, some in a more straightforward way and some in a more convoluted way. Aspects typically describe how a verb extends over time. The aspects in Baphian are the perfect, the imperfect, the habitual, the continuative, and the conditional. How each aspect is said on the verb is dependant on the tense of the verb. A reference table will be shown at the end of this section.

The perfect aspect is for an event that has been completed, is guaranteed to be completed, or a cyclical guarantee depending on the tense. In the gnomic tense with the perfect aspect, it is generally interpreted as a way of saying a cyclical thing that is guaranteed, but not in the habitual sense, it is more for planetary movements or quirky mecha anime. In the past and present tense it is a normal perfect tense. In the future tense it is similar to the gnomic, but not in a cyclical sense.

The imperfect is for an event that has not been completed, or is in the process of being completed (see imperfective). In the gnomic tense, the imperfect is for events that are “bound by fate” to not be completed. This can be used sarcastically, of course. In the past and present tense it is for events that are in the process of being completed. In the future tense, it is for events that should be completed in the future.
